# On discovering the hidden powers of writing

> September 19, 2026

So, for as long as I can remember, I’ve always been captivated by the power of language signs (whether those are words, hand signs, electrical impulses, light, etc.) and how they can impact both ends of the bridge that we call “communication.” Whether you’re the receiver or the sender, somehow the code being used affects everyone through meaning, intonation, intention, background, and so forth.

Fascinating!

We can communicate through just about anything, really, and we’re not the sole beings that communicate in our world. We’re constantly communicating with other animals, nature, and the whole universe. That’s such a bright perspective from which to view the world. It makes one wander freely into the extensibility of communication and how far we can go with it.

Well, I’ve always enjoyed the communication bits of my life, but I guess that enjoyment just started becoming clearer to me once I was able to put some intentionality into it. Let’s say I was once an unintentional communicator, and I’ve been working on changing that little by little ever since I was in school. Being introduced to the structure of my own language and the perspective of different languages, exploring how I change language registers depending on where I am, whom I’m talking with, and whom I’m writing to—all of that was really an eye-opening experience as to the extent of communication's impact on my life.

Later on, when I started my studies/career as a software student/developer, I began to grasp a whole new level of carefulness with communication. I was quickly introduced to the concepts of programming languages, technical documentation, software design, and all sorts of technical writing that I’m required/compelled to do. The challenges are indeed different, but the premises remain the same: for a clear communication path, you, the sender, have to transmit your intentionality, meaning, and structure in a way, in a register, and in a code that is transparent to your receiver—whether your receiver is a machine, a human, or the universe. Be effective and clear.

So, during my journey of finding intentionality in my communication and becoming a more resourceful software engineer, I came across many people who consider intentional and soulful writing an important activity for a software professional to unlock. Thankfully, I stumbled across many of those texts, like *Why improve your writing?* by Michael Lynch, *Why Engineers Need to Write* by Ryan Peterman, pieces and concepts of books such as *The Clean Coder* by Robert C. Martin, and so forth.

What I learned from them and from my worldview is what inspired me to start writing here and what I’m here to share with you. These points lean more toward the epistemological impact of writing upon oneself, meaning I won’t be covering points such as how writing can be used for commercial gains, portfolios, and so forth.

### Writing makes you own your thoughts

Owning your own thoughts is oftentimes harder than most people think. Sometimes you’re just too automated in the tasks you usually do, and you don’t take proper time to collect yourself and think critically about what you’re doing. If you want to test it out, take some time to just explain the complexity of the tasks you accomplish daily to someone; you will probably be surprised by the number of questions you’ll ask yourself, or that your questioner will ask you during that process, like “What is that for?” or “Why do I/you choose to go this path rather than that one?”

Writing your thought process down, even without a proper setting or format, is really clarifying and slows the pace of all the racing thoughts that come up in your head. You’re able to then delegate to a physical or virtual space the consuming task of keeping that disorganized thought inside your brain, and that’s really freeing.

After writing it down, you can better spot problems and weaknesses in your plan, thought, or speech. You can also see the potential of what you have thought of. It’s a valuable skill to acquire in whichever field you work in.

The power of writing to solidify messy ideas became obvious when I tried to define what I actually do for a living. It’s a very common question to be raised in social circles, and it can come from all ages and all different backgrounds. I never know if the person I’m talking to fully understands my job as a software engineer, so whether I should give a more technical response or a more general response was never clear to me. However, I’ve come to accept my responses better once I wrote them down and read them back to myself. That provided me with a feeling of resolution; I know that it is just about enough information to continue the conversation or to end it as needed.

### You get to know yourself better

Writing will reveal things about you that you never noticed before. It can show you new preferences you didn’t know you had, new dislikes you might not have noticed before, and recurring linguistic patterns you didn’t notice you had. It’s powerful, and it can give you a real mirror experience, which can be positively or negatively shocking.

That mirror puts you into critical thinking mode. You’re then positioned far away emotionally from the situation you’re writing about, and that’s very important for separating strong emotions from important decisions. You’ll view the world from a third-person perspective, bringing more neutrality to your decision-making.

In turn, being in a viewer's perspective can also take you on a journey of empathy, making you glance at how the other person is perceiving the situation. You can then talk with more sensitivity and care for others.

### You can scale your knowledge

Writing allows you to decouple your time from your expertise by turning repetitive answers into reusable assets. Documenting those answers gives me the capability of providing the same content with the same consistency at the same time to different people, and the only limit is the number of times that file can be shared :smile: .

### It deepens your understanding

Writing forces you to face your own knowledge gaps. It’s an uncompromising test of how deep your mental models actually go. You can’t just delegate the intermediate steps or rely on intuition when you have to articulate a problem line by line; every gap in your logic suddenly stares back at you on the screen/paper.

This is why you surely cannot rely on an LLM to tighten your understanding of something. While it can, for sure, give you easier access to knowledge and simplify/accelerate the learning process, it won’t magically enter your brain and make those synapses for you; that’s your job :).
